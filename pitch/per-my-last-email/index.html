One game — for publishers

Per My Last Email

You are the newest hire on floor four. The account you were given is not new — it has a sent folder, it has drafts, and it belonged to E. Vale, who is no longer with the company. Ten business days of email in which you choose what you say, and then choose who sees it.

The concept

what the game is

A corporate-uncanny thriller told entirely through one work laptop. No faces, no offices, no streets — only what renders on the screen. Ten business days at Bellwether & Locke, and the whole game is an inbox.

Every reply is two decisions, and the second one is the real one. First the tone — warm, flat or pointed, and what you are willing to put in writing at all. Then the routing: Reply, Reply-All, Forward, CC, BCC. Who is now a witness. Routings are permanent, they are recorded in your personnel file, and a CC that seemed polite on Day 2 is evidence on Day 9. There is no unsend.

The design rule the whole script is linted against: the game never resolves whether the firm is haunted, criminal, or a process that no longer needs the people executing it. Every artefact has to survive all three readings. If a mail only works as a ghost story, or only as a cover-up, or only as automation, it gets rewritten until all three stand.

The loop

one thread, start to finish
The day’s mail lands

A thread opens on the laptop. The interface is the client rather than a menu over it, and the story is read in the shape it would really arrive in.

You choose what to say

Warm, flat or pointed — and, more often than the game lets you feel comfortable about, whether to put it in writing at all.

You choose who sees it

Reply, Reply-All, Forward, CC, BCC. This is the actual mechanic. Adding a third address changes what other people are willing to write in that thread — and your manager is measurably a different person the moment someone is CC’d.

It is filed, permanently

Routings go into a personnel file you cannot edit. The game keeps the receipt and shows it to you later, in someone else’s hands.

Tomorrow is shaped by the thread, not by a menu

Ten days on one rising line. The account predates you and will survive you, and some of what happens is plausibly the account acting rather than you. It is never confirmed which.

The routing is permanent. Someone is still reading.

Modes and scope

what ships
Single-player, ten business days

One campaign, roughly two to four hours. No multiplayer, no procedural generation, no live service.

Six endings, off two axes you never see

Two Weeks, The Promotion, Regulatory Affairs, Restructured, Inbox Zero and Retained. The last one is the horror: your resignation could not be delivered, and the building does not accept this.

Eleven locales at launch

Every locale will carry the whole script, not a translated menu over English prose; four already do. The player’s typed name is handled through a sentinel that is never inflected, so no locale has to bend a stranger’s name into a case system.

Characters

voices in a mailbox, not portraits
The account — the real protagonist

You are an unnamed new hire; the thing with a history is the mailbox. Vale’s threads, drafts and sent items are your inherited memory and the story’s archive. The narration never jokes — the company is funny, the voice is a court reporter.

Dagny Voss — your manager

Warm in a one-to-one thread and glacial the moment anyone is CC’d, and the shift is measurable: contractions, first names and exclamation marks all vanish. The player can weaponise it. She never says the name Vale — only “your predecessor”, “the prior occupant of the role”.

Priya, Marcus and Tomás — the floor

People & Culture, a peer in Ops, and an IT helpdesk that exists only as ticket signatures. Each is a voice fingerprint rather than a portrait, and the fingerprint is the localisation contract: it must survive translation as register, not as wording.

E. Vale — your predecessor

Never seen, never explained, and never confirmed to be gone. Everything you know about Vale you read in a folder you inherited, which is exactly the position the game wants you in.

A work laptop and nothing else: a client, a calendar, a policy page, a personnel file. The chrome stays flat and administrative while the content goes wrong, so the horror arrives in a font nobody chose to be frightening.
